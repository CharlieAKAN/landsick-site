L.A. Cuts Red Tape for Small Shoots

The news

On October 27, 2026, the L.A. City Council passed two film-friendly measures meant to make local shoots cheaper and easier.

The first move creates free micro-shoot permits for very small productions. That means public-space shoots with 10 or fewer people, built for students, doc crews, creators, and low-budget filmmakers.

The second move cuts many city-owned parking and staging fees to $1 a day. Before this, FilmLA listed some fees at $450 a day for park base camp parking and $100 to $300 a day for crew parking.

Why we care

This is not just city paperwork. This is about who gets to make work.

A tiny crew should not need a mountain of cash just to shoot a walk-and-talk, a short doc scene, or a scrappy spec piece in the city that still sells itself as the home of movies.

For us, the lesson is simple: lower friction creates more reps. More reps create better filmmakers. Better filmmakers build stronger sets, stronger crews, and stronger stories.

The real problem under the good news

L.A. is doing this because production is still soft. FilmLA reported that Greater Los Angeles had 4,171 shoot days from July through September 2026, down 4.8% from the same time last year. That is not a small signal. That is a warning light.

California tax credits are helping some bigger shows, but small productions need a different kind of help. They need simple permits, clear rules, cheap parking, and a city that does not make them feel like a problem before they even roll camera.

What small crews should watch next

This is not a free-for-all. The original micro-shoot plan was built around low-impact work: handheld cameras, phones, small tripods, no big lights, no blocked sidewalks, no drones, no weapons, no stunts, and no special effects.

So if you are planning a tiny shoot in L.A., do not just wing it. Watch for the final rollout, read the permit rules, and keep your footprint clean. The whole point is to make small filmmaking easier without making neighborhoods hate film crews.

Our take

This is a small move, but it is pointed in the right direction.

Hollywood does not rebuild from the top down only. It rebuilds when crews can go shoot. When students can test ideas. When doc teams can follow the truth. When young directors can fail, learn, and try again without burning half the budget on red tape.

If L.A. wants to stay a film town, it has to make room for the next wave. This week, it finally made that room a little wider.
