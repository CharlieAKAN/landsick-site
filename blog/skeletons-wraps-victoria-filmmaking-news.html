Skeletons Wraps in Victoria

A Hollywood Thriller Just Wrapped in Victoria

Here’s the filmmaking story worth our attention this week: Skeletons, a new psychological thriller starring Brie Larson, has wrapped production in Victoria, Australia.

The film comes from director JT Mollner, who made Strange Darling, with J.J. Abrams producing. The shoot used Melbourne, regional Victoria, and The Studio Forest Hill, the reactivated Nunawading studio that used to house Neighbours.

Why We Care

This is not just another casting headline. This is a production story. The big takeaway is simple: the right location package can shape the whole film.

Skeletons moved through places like the Yarra Valley, Dandenong Ranges, Mornington Peninsula, and Werribee. That gives the filmmakers a wide visual range without needing to fake every world on a stage.

That matters for us. When we scout, we are not just looking for something pretty. We are looking for texture, control, access, sound, light, parking, crew base, and the feeling the camera can steal from a real place.

The Studio Move Is the Real Signal

The coolest part is that Skeletons was the first film to use The Studio Forest Hill after its reactivation. That is a big deal because old production spaces do not always come back to life. When they do, they give crews a new home base.

VicScreen says the production created an estimated 635 jobs for local cast and crew, including 14 heads of department and 50 post-production and VFX roles. It also put more than $21.5 million into the state economy and worked with around 300 local businesses.

That is the part most people miss. A film set is not only directors, actors, and cameras. It is builders, drivers, medics, caterers, hotels, gear houses, editors, VFX artists, and the small vendors who keep the machine moving.

What This Means for Filmmakers

We should take this as a reminder: production value is often built before the first shot.

A strong location can save money. A nearby crew base can save time. A practical studio can give you control. A region with trained local crew can keep your shoot from getting messy.

For smaller teams, the lesson is the same. Do not just ask, “Does this place look good?” Ask, “Can we actually make the day here?”

That question changes everything. It affects the schedule. It affects the lighting plan. It affects sound. It affects morale. And morale shows up on screen, whether we like it or not.

Our Take

Skeletons is a reminder that filmmaking is local, even when the names are global.

Brie Larson and J.J. Abrams might make the headline, but the real engine is the crew, the locations, and the production system underneath the frame. That is where movies either hold together or fall apart.

So this week, we are looking at our own projects and asking a clean question: where can we make the location do more work for the story?
